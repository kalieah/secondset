Need it or Want it?

Why do we buy things we actually don't need?

There are so many reasons you might walk into a store. Maybe you actually need something, maybe you’re just looking around, or maybe you just got "drawn in". But somehow, you always end up leaving with more than you came for. “Wait, is this on sale?” “Let me just take a look.” and suddenly, you’re going home with a bunch of stuff you never actually needed.

So, why does this happen?

The answer is far more complex than simply saying, "I just couldn't resist." Our spending habits are influenced by factors such as psychology, marketing, social pressure, and, of course, the economy.

Needs vs. Wants

We already know the difference between a need and a want. You need food. You don't exactly need the new shoes you've been looking at for the past two weeks.

But here's the interesting part: knowing you don't need something doesn't make you stop wanting it.

We don't only buy things because they're necessary. We buy things because they're convenient, enjoyable, good-looking, useful, or because they somehow feel like us. Sometimes, we even buy things simply because we happened to see them at the right moment.

That means a product can have value to us without being something we actually need. And that's where things get a little more interesting.

The Value We See Isn't Always the Price

Imagine two almost identical T-shirts. One costs $15. The other costs $60 because it comes from a popular brand. Now, you might look at them and think, “There's no way the second one is four times better.” and maybe it isn't.

So why do people still pay the extra $45?

Because we're not only paying for the physical product. We're also paying for the way we perceive it.

A brand can make something feel more desirable through its reputation, design, exclusivity, or what owning it says about us.

The T-shirt didn't magically become better. Our perception of its value changed and in economics, that matters.

Scarcity Makes Us Want Things More

You've probably seen phrases like “Only 3 left,” “Limited edition,” or “Sale ends tonight.” and suddenly, something you weren't even thinking about buying starts looking pretty tempting.

That's scarcity.

When something seems difficult to get—or like it might disappear soon—we often place more value on it. The product itself hasn't changed. The question has.

Instead of asking, “Do I actually want this?” we're suddenly thinking, “What if I don't get it now?”

And that's a very different decision.

A limited-edition product isn't necessarily better than the regular one. But knowing that we might not get another chance can make it feel much harder to leave behind.

The Cost Isn't Just What You Pay

There's another idea hiding behind every purchase: opportunity cost.

Say you spend $50 on something you didn't really need. The cost isn't just the $50. It's also what that $50 could have been used for instead.

You could have saved it, used it for something you actually needed later, or spent it on an experience.

Once you spend the money, those alternatives are gone. That's opportunity cost.

Every time we choose one option, we're giving up another. So the real cost of a purchase isn't always what's written on the price tag. Sometimes, it's what you could have done with that money instead.

We're Influenced by Other People

We like to think our shopping decisions are completely our own. But look around.

If everyone suddenly starts wearing the same shoes, using the same phone, or talking about the same product, it probably doesn't take long before you start noticing it too.

And once you notice it, you might start wanting it.

Social media makes this even harder to ignore. We're constantly seeing what other people are buying, wearing, recommending, and apparently loving.

Sometimes we don't just want the product. We want what comes with owning the product.

Maybe it's feeling trendy. Maybe it's fitting in. Maybe it's feeling like you're part of something.

And sometimes, without even realizing it, we're not buying because we decided we wanted something. We're buying because we've been shown that it's something worth wanting.

So, Are We Making Irrational Decisions?

Not necessarily.

Buying something you don't technically need doesn't automatically mean it was a bad decision.

Economics isn't about always choosing the cheapest option. It's about the value we expect to get from our choices.

If buying a new game gives you hours of entertainment, that has value. If a more expensive jacket is better quality and lasts longer, that has value too.

The important question isn't always, “Do I need this?” It can also be, “What value am I actually getting from this?”

And maybe even, “Is that value worth what I'm giving up?”

Because sometimes we buy something because we genuinely think it's worth the money.

And sometimes we buy it because a discount, a trend, or a “limited edition” label made us feel like we had to.

Those might look like the same purchase from the outside. Economically, they're not quite the same decision.

So the next time you buy something you didn't plan to buy, pause for a second.

Maybe you were attracted by the discount. Maybe everyone else seemed to have it. Maybe it suddenly felt scarce. Or maybe you simply decided that you liked it enough to pay for it.

Whatever the reason, there's probably more economics behind that little decision than you think.

Because economics isn't just about money. It's about understanding the choices we make—and why we make them.
