The Price of Being Late

Are we paying for the product—or for the time we don't have?

You wake up late. You have ten minutes to get ready, and suddenly the bus that normally gets you to school for almost nothing is no longer an option. You open a ride-hailing app. The price is higher than you expected.

You pay it anyway.

Technically, you just paid for a ride. But economically, you paid for something else too: the time you didn't have.

Time Has a Price

We usually think about prices in terms of money. A coffee costs ₺100. A taxi costs ₺400. A plane ticket costs ₺3,000. But not every cost appears on a receipt.

Imagine you have two ways to get somewhere. The bus takes 45 minutes, while a taxi takes 15. If you're not in a hurry, the bus might be the obvious choice. But imagine you're already late. Suddenly, the calculation changes.

The taxi hasn't necessarily become a better taxi. The bus hasn't necessarily become a worse bus. What changed was the value of your time.

This is where economics gets interesting.

The Cost of Choosing

Economists use the term opportunity cost to describe what you give up when you choose one option over another. If you spend two hours watching a movie, the opportunity cost isn't necessarily the price of the movie. It's what else you could have done with those two hours.

The same idea applies to being late.

Choosing the bus might save you money, but it costs you time. Choosing the taxi costs more money, but it saves time. So when you're in a hurry, the question isn't simply, "Which option is cheaper?"

It becomes:

"What is my time worth right now?"

And that answer can change from one moment to another.

Paying for Convenience

This is also why we sometimes pay more for convenience.

Think about food delivery. You could walk to a restaurant, wait, pick up your food, and come back. Or you could pay extra to have it delivered to your door.

You're not necessarily paying because the food itself is better. You're paying to avoid spending your own time on the trip.

The same thing happens with express shipping, same-day delivery, taxis, and other services that save time. In a way, these services are selling us something that can't be manufactured or stored: time.

And when we don't have much of it, we may be willing to pay more for it.

When Time Becomes Scarce

Being late can also change the choices available to you. When you have plenty of time, you can compare prices, wait for public transportation, walk, or choose a slower option.

When you're running out of time, many of those choices disappear.

Your willingness to pay can increase because the alternative has become more costly.

The taxi isn't just competing with the bus anymore. It's competing with the consequences of being late.

And sometimes, those consequences feel much more expensive than the taxi ride.

The Same Service, Different Value

This idea appears in places we might not immediately notice. A plane ticket bought months in advance can cost much less than the same flight booked at the last minute. An ordinary delivery might be cheap, while express delivery costs considerably more.

In each case, speed can become valuable because time has become scarce.

The interesting part is that scarcity doesn't only apply to products. It can apply to our time, too.

When something becomes scarce, we tend to value it more. And unlike a product on a shelf, you can't simply produce more time when you run out.

So, What Are We Actually Paying For?

The next time you pay extra for a taxi because you're late, think about what you're actually buying.

Maybe it's transportation. Maybe it's convenience. Maybe it's fifteen extra minutes. Or maybe it's the chance to avoid being late.

The price on the screen tells you how much money you're spending. It doesn't tell you the value of the time you're saving.

Sometimes, the most expensive thing isn't what we buy.

It's the time we don't have.
